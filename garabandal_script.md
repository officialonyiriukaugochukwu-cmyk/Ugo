A twelve-year-old girl is walking backward down a mountain path at night. Fast. Her head is thrown back, her eyes fixed on a patch of empty sky, and the ground under her feet is loose stone the size of fists.

She does not stumble. She does not glance behind her. Not once.

Around her, adults are struggling to keep up: farmers, a few doctors, a handful of priests who came to prove she was faking. They are the ones tripping.

This happened again and again in a Spanish village in 1961, and the central irony is this. The Catholic Church has never said it was false. It has also never said it was true. For more than sixty years, one of the most closely watched apparition cases of the twentieth century has sat in a kind of sealed pause.

And the pause traces back to a single date that nobody has announced yet. A date that, according to the story, only one living person is supposed to know.

So how does a case stay unresolved for sixty years? Well, there are three reasons. And the third is the one that almost never makes it into the retelling.

But first, the village.

San Sebastián de Garabandal sits in the mountains of Cantabria, in northern Spain. In 1961 it was a farming hamlet of a few hundred people, reached by a rough track, where the biggest weekly event was Sunday Mass.

Four girls lived there, all around eleven or twelve. Conchita, Mari Loli, Jacinta, and Mari Cruz. Three of them shared a surname that was common in the village, and not much else. Conchita's father had died, and she was being raised by her widowed mother.

Nothing about them was remarkable. And that is what the story depends on.

On June 18, 1961, the four were doing something that does not appear on any holy card. They were stealing apples from the schoolmaster's orchard.

They said that a sound like thunder stopped them. And then they said they saw a figure of light. An angel.

Fifteen days later, on July 2, 1961, they said the angel was followed by a woman in white with a brown mantle, who they came to understand was the Virgin Mary under the title of Our Lady of Mount Carmel.

Word moved faster than the road.

Within weeks, the village was filling with strangers. And what the strangers watched was the trance. The girls would drop to their knees together, mid-sentence. They stared upward. They spoke to someone no one else could hear. Witnesses reported that they seemed not to feel pinches, pins, or the blazing glare of a flashlight held to their eyes.

Then the girls started to talk about messages.

On October 18, 1961, they delivered the first. It called for sacrifice and penance, in plain language, to a world that had not asked.

On July 18, 1962, a crowd gathered to watch Conchita in ecstasy. She extended her tongue, and observers said a white host appeared on it, with no hand anywhere near. The moment was photographed. The photographs spread across Europe.

And in the years that followed, the messages grew harder.

On June 18, 1965, a second message arrived, and it was aimed squarely at the Church itself. The cup, it said, was filling. Too many priests, bishops, and cardinals were walking the wrong road. It was not a message addressed to pilgrims. It was addressed to the institution that would have to judge it.

Five months later, on November 13, 1965, the apparitions ended. By the girls' account, the last one was the last one.

But what remained was a promise.

The girls said they had been told of three future events. A worldwide moment of interior conscience, usually called the Warning. A permanent, visible sign at the pine trees above the village. And a conditional chastisement, if the world ignored all of it.

The sign is the part that matters here. According to Conchita, the date would be revealed to her alone, eight days beforehand. It would fall on the feast of a young saint who died defending the Eucharist. And it would happen on a Thursday evening.

Hold on to that. It is the key to everything that follows.

Now, the Church. Who actually investigates a claim like this?

The answer, under the system in place at the time, was the local bishop. Not Rome. The bishop of Santander appointed a commission of priests, theologians, and medical consultants, and over the early 1960s they interviewed the girls, questioned the villagers, and visited the site.

Their conclusion, endorsed under Bishop Eugenio Beitia, was a Latin phrase: non constat de supernaturalitate.

It does not mean "it was fake."

Here is what it means. It means the supernatural character of the events has not been established. That's all. Under the old system, there were three possible findings. Constat de supernaturalitate: it is established. Non constat: it is not established. And constat de non supernaturalitate: it is established that it is not. Garabandal received the middle one.

Think of it as a verdict of "unproven." Not guilty, not innocent, not closed.

And there is something else the process does not mean, even in the best case. Even when a bishop says yes, no Catholic is required to believe. These are what the Church calls private revelations. A ruling can say a devotion is safe to practice. It cannot make anyone's faith depend on it.

So the Garabandal ruling was narrow. Yet the way it was received was not.

Because the commission did not stop at silence. It recorded concerns. Inconsistencies among the girls' accounts. Natural explanations for the trances that some physicians had floated. And, for the supporters, the part that stung most: the bishop's team had spent years in the village, and still declined to affirm.

That brings us to the three reasons.

The first reason is the simplest, and it is structural. The heart of the Garabandal case is not something that happened. It is something that is going to happen.

A commission can examine a trance. It can interview a witness. It cannot certify a prophecy that has not come due. If the Church approved Garabandal and the sign never came, it would have vouched for a failed promise. If it condemned Garabandal and the sign did come, it would have condemned a true one.

Either ruling is a bet against a clock.

The second reason is the paper trail. And here the story is genuinely tangled.

Critics point to statements attributed to the girls, in the years after, that sound like retractions or doubts. Supporters answer that those statements were mistranscribed, taken out of context, or pulled from children under pressure. Both sides cite documents. The documents do not agree. And the people who wrote them are, in several cases, no longer alive to clarify.

When the evidence is that contested, an institution built on caution has an easy answer: wait.

But there's another reason, and it's more interesting.

The third reason is that nobody inside the system has anything to gain by ruling.

The original verdict belonged to one bishop, in one era. Every bishop of Santander since has inherited a file in which the people who could be hurt by reopening it outnumber the people who could be helped. Reopen it, and you invite crowds, controversy, and a possible second "unproven." Close it, and you lose the faithful for whom the file has become sacred.

So the file stays where it is.

Now for the complication. Because in the late 1960s, the case acquired a name that supporters could not stop saying.

Padre Pio.

The stigmatic friar from southern Italy was, by then, one of the most famous religious figures alive. And a story spread among supporters that he believed in Garabandal, that the girls had met him or at least written to him, and that he had expressed his support.

It is a compelling story. It's also, so far as the public record shows, a story told through intermediaries rather than a signed endorsement.

Padre Pio died on September 23, 1968.

And that left a strange gap. If he was an ally, the ally was gone. If he was a rumor, the rumor had outlived its subject. Either way, the Church had nothing from him to consider.

Meanwhile, the girls grew up. They left the village. Conchita eventually married an American and moved abroad, where she lived a quiet family life far from the pines. The four girls who once had thousands of eyes on them became, over decades, four middle-aged women with ordinary lives.

Except one thing hadn't changed. Conchita was still understood to hold the date.

Which brings us to the present.

On May 17, 2024, the Vatican's Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith published new norms for judging alleged supernatural phenomena. And the new rules changed the vocabulary.

Gone is the old three-way Latin verdict. In its place are six possible outcomes, ranging from a statement that there is nothing objectionable to a formal declaration that a phenomenon is not supernatural. The Vatican said, plainly, that it would now seldom if ever declare an apparition to be supernatural. The highest ruling available is, in effect, a permission.

And the new system asks Rome, not just the local bishop, to take a direct role.

So where does that leave Garabandal?

In a place no one planned for. It carries a verdict from an older system that no longer exists. It has never been reviewed under the new one. Pilgrims still arrive. The pines are still marked. And a bishop's old three words still sit above it all, unchanged.

Here is the thing a skeptic would say at this point. Surely the Church simply decided it was false, and everyone is being polite.

But the record does not say that. The record says "not established." And that distinction is not politeness. It is the entire point.

Because there is a deeper question underneath the paperwork. What is an institution for, when the claim in front of it cannot be tested until the future arrives?

The Church has spent centuries refining how it judges the past. A miracle, a healing, a vision. Things that happened, and left evidence behind. But Garabandal asks it to judge a story that is still waiting for its ending. And that is a different kind of question.

If the sign came tomorrow, the Church would have to explain why it hesitated. If it never comes, the believers would have to explain why they waited. And in either case, the people who spent their lives on a mountain path, walking backward in the dark, would have been waiting on something no commission could examine.

So here is what's left.

If a claim can only be judged after it is proven true or false, and the institution responsible for judging it will not move until then, who exactly is the ruling for? Is the silence a form of protection, or a form of abandonment? And if the date finally comes, and the people who waited for it are gone, who will have kept faith with whom?
